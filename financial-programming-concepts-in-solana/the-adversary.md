# Chapter 9: The Adversary

APIs churn; exploits recur. The same half-dozen attack classes have drained onchain markets for a decade, on every chain, in every framework, and they will drain the next decade's markets too. That makes this the most durable section in the book, and the one to reread before every launch. Each class below ends with the defense, and where a real incident makes the lesson concrete, a War Story tells it.

## Every Mistake Is a Bounty, Claimable by Anyone

Deploying financial software on a public chain means accepting a threat model no other kind of software faces. Every instruction handler is callable by anyone on earth, pseudonymously, for a fraction of a cent. Your source, or at least your bytecode, is public. And every mistake is not a crash report but a bounty: a mispriced trade, a leaked minor unit, an unchecked account is money, claimable by the first person to notice.

Three consequences shape everything:

- **Attacks are industrialized.** A flaw worth a cent per call meets a loop that calls it a million times. Nothing is too small to steal; judge every leak by what it pays at full throughput.
- **Attackers see your transaction before it lands.** Ordering on a public chain is contestable: a pending swap can be sandwiched (front-run to move the price against it, then back-run to profit), and any profitable pending action can be copied and raced. This is MEV, extractable value from ordering, and it is why every trade in this book carries a caller-side bound: minimum output, maximum slippage. The program cannot promise the state it will execute against; it can only refuse to execute against a state worse than the user accepted.
- **The boundary values are where the money is.** Zero amounts, empty pools, dust quantities, exact thresholds: the adversary lives at the edges of your ranges, because that is where comparisons flip and remainders round. Every class below is, at bottom, an edge case someone weaponized.

## Validation: Signers, Owners, and the Confused Deputy

The most exploited bug class in Solana's history is not exotic math; it is a program believing an account it should have checked. Callers supply the accounts an instruction runs against, so *every* account is a claim, and validation is the act of checking each claim against something the program already trusts: a signature, an owner, a stored address, a derivation.

The full checklist is short. Is the account that authorizes this action actually a **signer**? Is each account **owned** by the program that should own it, so its data can be believed? Does each account **match the one recorded** when the relationship was created (the `has_one` pattern: the vault recorded on the market, the maker recorded on the offer)? And is each PDA **derived** from the seeds it claims, with its stored bump? Anchor's constraints express all four declaratively, and the program chapters use them on every account of every instruction.

The attack that punishes a missing check is **substitution**, and its classic form has a name from the 1988 security literature: the **confused deputy**, an entity with authority tricked into using it on an attacker's behalf. Your program is the deputy: it holds the vault keys, and the attacker's goal is to make it sign a transfer while looking at the wrong accounts. Pass your own account where the payment destination belongs. Pass the fee vault where the user vault belongs (the two are the same mint and the same authority; only a stored-address check tells them apart, which is why the order book's `has_one` constraints exist). Pass a lookalike data account you fabricated, full of numbers the program will trust.

> **War Story: Cashio, $52M, 2022.** Cashio's stablecoin was minted against deposited collateral, and the mint instruction handler validated its accounts in a chain: this account is trusted because that one vouches for it. One link, the collateral token's mint data, went unvalidated: the "root of trust" account was never checked against anything the program knew. An attacker fabricated the whole chain, hung it from a worthless token, deposited nothing of value, and minted $52M of stablecoin against it. Every link in an account chain must terminate in something the program itself derived or recorded; a chain that vouches for itself proves nothing.

> **War Story: Wormhole, $325M, 2022.** The Wormhole bridge accepted a "verify signatures" result account as proof that guardians had approved a message. The instruction handler that created such accounts was meant to be the only source, but the check that the sysvar account passed to verification was the *real* sysvar used a deprecated, spoofable pattern. The attacker fabricated the verification account, presented it as proof, and minted 120,000 wrapped ETH backed by nothing. Verification you did not perform is verification that did not happen: proofs must be checked by the program consuming them, against accounts it validated itself.

## The Empty Pool: Inflation and Donation Attacks

Every share-based pool (vault, LP pool, lending reserve) prices shares as pool value divided by share supply, and that formula has a degenerate zone: the beginning, when both numbers are tiny. The **inflation attack** exploits it. The attacker makes the first deposit, minuscule, receiving a minuscule share supply. Then they *donate* assets directly into the pool's vault, without going through deposit, inflating the value behind each of their few shares. The next depositor's shares are computed as deposit × supply ÷ value, and with value enormous relative to supply, the division floors to zero, or nearly: the victim's deposit buys nothing, and the attacker's shares absorb it.

The defenses all attack the degenerate zone directly. **Lock a minimum liquidity** that is never minted to anyone, as the AMM does with its floor of 100, so share supply can never be dust-sized relative to the pool. **Use virtual shares and virtual assets**: add a constant to both sides of the exchange-rate division so the ratio is well-conditioned even when real supply is zero. **Make donations inert**: track pool size in program state (the lending market's `available_liquidity`) rather than reading the vault's token balance, so tokens pushed into the vault sideways simply do not count. And **require a meaningful first deposit**. One of these, deliberately chosen, is enough; the programs to worry about are the ones (like the book's own vault strategy, which says so honestly) that rely on circumstance instead of a named defense.

## A True Price Can Still Be a Purchased Price

Everything in Offchain Truth assumed the fact was honest, and its checks (publisher, freshness, confidence) defend against *fake* facts. This class is nastier: the fact is real, fresh, and correctly verified, and it is an accurate report of a market the attacker just bent. Push a thin market's price up for one slot, and every program consuming that price acts faithfully on it: lending markets extend credit against inflated collateral, exchanges settle at fantasy prices, liquidations fire on positions that were healthy a block ago.

> **War Story: Mango Markets, $116M, 2022.** An attacker opened large perpetual positions in Mango's own thinly-traded MNGO token, then bought MNGO aggressively on the spot markets the oracle read from, moving the price up more than twenty-fold in minutes. The oracle reported the manipulated price accurately. Mango's risk engine, believing it, marked the attacker's positions massively profitable, and the attacker borrowed $116M of the platform's deposits against that unrealized profit and left. The attacker's own description, "a highly profitable trading strategy," became the class's dark punchline: no signature was forged and no account spoofed; the input was true and the truth had been purchased.

The defenses are about making purchased truth expensive and slow. **Time-weight the price**: a **time-weighted average price** (TWAP) over minutes forces the attacker to hold the manipulation, at cost, against arbitrage, instead of renting one block of it. **Respect the confidence interval**: a manipulated thin market shows its stress as widened confidence, and a consumer that degrades (pausing borrows, tightening limits) when confidence widens closes the profitable window. **Bound per-block state change**: caps on how much can be borrowed or withdrawn per window turn a one-shot drain into a multi-block campaign that defenders can see. And the bluntest, most effective one: **do not build leverage on a market thinner than the credit it collateralizes.** If the cost of moving the price is less than what the move unlocks, that trade will eventually be made.

## A Rounding Error Is a Faucet Once Someone Finds the Loop

The Arithmetic of Money set the policy: round against the user, floor once, ceil debt. This class is what happens to programs that did not. Each favorable rounding is sub-cent; the exploit is the loop, and the loop's favorite venue is an **empty or near-empty market**, where the attacker controls both sides of the ratio and can shape the divisor to maximize what flooring forgives.

> **War Story: the Hundred and Sonne drains, $7M and $20M.** Hundred Finance (2023) and Sonne Finance (2024), both forks of the same lending design on EVM chains, fell to the same sequence: find or create a freshly-listed, empty market; make a tiny deposit to mint dust-level shares; donate a large sum to skew the exchange rate (the inflation setup); then exploit the rate calculation's rounding direction, redeeming shares sized so the floored share-burn undercounted by one, repeatedly, until the pool drained. Two lending markets, a year apart, with the first post-mortem public the whole time: exploit classes recur because the underlying arithmetic ships again in every fork.

The defense is The Arithmetic of Money applied without exception, plus one test-suite habit: a **round-trip property test**. Deposit then immediately withdraw, swap in then swap back, at adversarial sizes (one minor unit, pool-sized, prime-numbered), and assert the caller never ends with more than they started. The lending example's `deposit_redeem_round_trip_creates_no_value` is eleven lines, and it is the test that would have failed on both protocols above.

## Ordering and Composability: CPI Trust Boundaries

A transaction can invoke your program, which invokes another, which invokes another. Composability is Solana's superpower (Solana Building Blocks made that case), and every composition is also a trust boundary: mid-transaction, between your instructions, or inside a CPI you make, code you did not write gets to run.

The discipline is **checks-effects-interactions**: validate everything, then update your own state, and only then call out to other programs or move tokens. The order book's settlement zeroes a user's claimed balance *before* the transfer, so even if the token transfer could re-enter (token extensions can attach hooks to transfers), a re-entrant call finds the balance already spent. Solana's runtime forbids re-entering a program through another program, the pattern that plagued EVM contracts (only direct self-recursion is allowed, capped in depth), but the discipline earns its keep anyway, because it also defends against the failure you cannot see coming: a callee that is upgraded tomorrow, a hook added to a mint next year, an instruction sequenced after yours in the same transaction.

Two more boundary rules. **Validate what a CPI returns and what it was given**: calling a program does not sanctify the accounts it touched; the vault strategy computes each swap's minimum output from the oracle precisely because the router it calls is outside its trust boundary. And **assume hostile sequencing**: any invariant your program needs must hold at the end of *your* instruction, not at the end of the transaction, because the instructions after yours belong to someone else. The postcondition asserts from The Arithmetic of Money (re-check K after the transfers land) are exactly this: your state, proven sound, before anyone else gets the keys.

## What You Learnt

- Every mistake on a public chain is a bounty: attacks are industrialized, ordering is contestable, and the boundary values are where the money is.
- Every supplied account is a claim to verify, and every trust chain must terminate in something the program itself derived or recorded.
- Empty pools need a named defense against inflation attacks, and rounding leaks become faucets the moment someone finds the loop.
- A true price can still be a purchased price: time-weight it, respect the confidence interval, bound per-block change, and never build leverage on a market thinner than the credit it collateralizes.
- Checks-effects-interactions at every boundary: your invariants must hold at the end of your own instruction, because the instructions after yours belong to someone else.
