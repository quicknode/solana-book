# Chapter 8: Offchain Truth

A program can verify a lot on its own: signatures, balances, account ownership, its own arithmetic. But most financial products depend on facts that happen in the real world, outside anything the chain can see. Did the Federal Reserve cut rates this afternoon? Which film grossed highest this month? Did the shipment arrive? Did the borrower's collateral just crash? Five of the book's programs turn on a fact like this: prices for the lending market, the perpetual futures exchange and the prop AMM, an event outcome for the betting market, asset valuations for the managed fund. They all reach the program the same way: someone the program trusts signs the fact with their private key, and the program checks that fact's provenance, freshness, and confidence, and rejects degenerate values, before it uses it.

## An Oracle's Publication Is an Input to Check

Publishers of signed facts are called **oracles**, and what an oracle publishes reaches a program in one of three shapes.

The first is **signed straight to you**: the trusted party signs the transaction carrying the fact with their private key. The betting market's admin settling an event is this, and the program checks a signer, and which public key signed.

The second is **published to an account**: the trusted party signs its statement offchain with their private key, an oracle program verifies that signature onchain and records the fact in an account it owns, and your program reads that account in a later transaction. Pyth Core and Chainlink both work this way, and your own program verifies no signature at all. The signature was checked already, in somebody else's transaction. What your program establishes is where the account came from.

The third is **signed offchain and carried in your transaction**: the trusted party signs its statement offchain with their private key, and the client fetches the signed statement and passes it to your program as an instruction argument. The signature is checked in that same transaction, by your program or by an oracle program it calls. Pyth Pro works this way. Nothing is stored, and the fact is used in the instruction that verified it. The client chooses which signed statement to submit, so it can submit an old one, and the freshness check below is what stops it.

Whichever the shape, by the time your program sees the fact it is an ordinary instruction argument or ordinary account data, and it can be held to the same standard as every other input:

- **The provenance.** Is this the exact account the program was configured with, owned by the program whose verification you are relying on? A fact from an unexpected account is rejected. Anchor's `Account<T>` enforces the owner and the discriminator, and the price feed's own identity is a stored-pubkey comparison on top of that. The managed fund rejects any price account that is not the exact price feed recorded in its registry. The lending market's example price feed is a test stand-in written by `set_price()`, which only a transaction signed with the market owner's private key may run; in production the reserve points at a Pyth `PriceUpdateV2` account, the second shape.
- **The freshness.** A true price from five minutes ago can be a dangerously wrong price now. Every consumer states its tolerance and rejects anything older: the lending market refuses prices more than 25 slots old, the managed fund more than 60 seconds. Which clock a tolerance is stated in, and why the answer is usually slots, is the *Time* chapter's subject.
- **The confidence.** Good price feeds publish their own uncertainty: $250 ± $0.10 is a different fact from $250 ± $8, and the second one, during a volatile minute, may not be safe to liquidate against. The managed fund refuses a price whose confidence interval is wider than 1% of the price (`OracleConfidenceTooWide`), and the perpetual futures exchange and prop AMM refuse one wider than the pool's `max_confidence_bps`. A fresh price with a wide confidence interval is a separate failure mode from a stale one.
- **The degenerate values.** A price of zero or below means a broken price feed, and the program must fail before that number multiplies against anything. Negative prices are real but exceptionally rare, and never for a token.[^negative-oil]

> **How a Pyth price reaches your program.** **Pyth Core**, the free price feeds that the managed fund reads and the other programs stand in for with a mock, is signed by Pyth's guardian set: five private keys held by Pyth, any three of whose signatures make a valid update.[^pyth-guardians] Anyone can post a signed update to Solana, where the **Pyth Receiver program** verifies those signatures and writes a `PriceUpdateV2` account that it owns. Your program checks the account's `feed_id`, `publish_time`, and `conf`, plus `verification_level`, which records whether three of the guardians signed or fewer, so a partially verified update is a weaker fact than a fully verified one. The companion repository's Pyth example (`basics/pyth`) checks the age from `publish_time` and reads `feed_id`, `conf` and `price`; the managed fund is the program that rejects a wide `conf` and a wrong price feed address.
>
> **Pyth Pro**, the paid low-latency product, streams its prices to your client, which receives each update as one signed message: the price, its confidence and timestamp, Pyth's signature made with a single private key, and the matching public key. The client then builds a transaction with two instructions. The first goes to Solana's built-in ed25519 program, which checks the signature over the message. The second goes to your program, with the signed message in its instruction data. Your program passes the message to the Pyth Pro verifier program, which checks that the ed25519 instruction covered exactly these bytes and that the public key is one Pyth has registered as trusted. It hands the price back for your program to check and use; changing one byte of the message breaks the signature, so the client that carried it cannot alter it. At most two public keys are registered at once, each with an expiry, so Pyth can replace one.

## Trust Is an Engineering Parameter

Who signs the fact is a design decision with a range of answers, and the way to make it is to ask three questions: who publishes, what does it cost them to lie, and what happens when they stop publishing?

At one end is the **single trusted signer**. The betting market's admin settles the event with one signature: `settle_event()` writes their answer to the event account's `winning_outcome_index`, and every payout follows from it. For a small market whose operator's business is its reputation, this can be the right design, and the program's job is to make the trust *visible and narrow*. The admin picks the winning outcome, and the program limits what else that signature can do, as the *Betting Market* chapter shows; a reader of the code can see the limit.

In the middle are **committees and multi-party resolution**: several parties must agree before a fact is recorded, so lying requires collusion and any single defector breaks the lie. The drawback is coordination and latency.

At the far end are **price feed networks** like Pyth, which aggregate many independent publishers (exchanges, market makers) into a single signed publication with a confidence interval, updated continuously. Lying requires bribing a meaningful share of publishers whose businesses depend on being right, which is the strongest economic answer available to "what does it cost them to lie." The publishers' signatures are checked offchain, where the network combines them; what reaches the chain is signed with the network's own private keys, three of five for Pyth Core and one for Pyth Pro. A program that reads Pyth trusts Pyth to keep those private keys safe as well as trusting its publishers, and a single stolen Pro private key could sign a false price until Pyth removes its public key. This is the production path for the lending market, the perpetual futures exchange and the prop AMM; in the companion repository all three read a test stand-in, and the managed fund reads the Pyth account layout itself. The companion repository's Pyth example decodes that account with the `pyth-solana-receiver-sdk` crate's `PriceUpdateV2` type; the checks in the previous section apply whichever network a program reads.

The third question, **what happens when publication stops**, needs an answer in the program's state machine. Price feeds go stale during outages; committees deadlock; admins lose private keys or interest. A program in which every way to withdraw needs a fresh fact strands everyone's funds when the source stops publishing. The staleness checks above stop the program acting on an old fact. The state machine has to cover the other case: every participant must be able to get their assets out of every state without a new fact arriving. The betting market's admin can call `cancel_event()`, after which each bettor takes their stake back with `claim_refund()`, without an outcome ever being declared. The lending market's `redeem_reserve_collateral()` reads no price feed, so suppliers can leave while the oracle is silent; only a borrower's collateral withdrawal, which needs a health check, waits for a price.[^escape-hatch] When you choose a trusted source, decide at the same time what the program does when it goes silent.

One more rule applies: **the fact's meaning must be fixed when participants commit their tokens, not when the fact arrives.** The betting market fixes its outcome list when the admin opens it to bets, before any bet can land, and its fee and betting close time earlier still, as the *Betting Market* chapter shows. The question bettors staked on cannot be rewritten before it is answered, and betting stops before the answer can be known.

The price-manipulation section of the *Adversary* chapter covers the case these checks cannot catch: a signed fact that is fresh, confident, correctly verified, and *engineered*, a true report of a market the attacker just manipulated.

## What You Learnt

- A program can act on a fact from outside the chain once someone it trusts has signed it with their private key. They can sign your transaction directly, sign offchain for an oracle program to verify and record in an account, or sign offchain for the client to carry into a transaction that checks the signature. Whichever the shape, the publication is input: check its provenance, its freshness (in the right clock), its confidence, and its degenerate values.
- Treat trust as an engineering parameter: choose a single signer, a committee, or a price feed network by asking who publishes, what lying costs them, and what happens when they go silent.
- Fix the fact's meaning when participants commit their tokens, and never let a silent oracle trap anyone: the *State Machines* chapter's rule applies without a fresh fact.
