# Writing Plan

How the manuscript gets produced, verified, reviewed, and kept alive. The table of contents lives in [TABLE-OF-CONTENTS.md](TABLE-OF-CONTENTS.md), and each chapter is a file under its section's folder. Within this document, chapters are referenced by name so reordering never breaks it; the manuscript's chapter titles carry sequential numbers ("Chapter 11: Escrow"), and the table of contents is the source of truth for the numbering.

## Principles

- The book prints the durable layer. Every code claim is verified against the companion repository before it lands: the skill's "grep before naming" rule, applied to a manuscript.
- Diagrams are the longest-lived medium. Every chapter budget includes them: account map, state machine, flow.
- Style is inherited wholesale from the [companion AI skill](https://github.com/quicknode/solana-finance-claude-plugin): onchain and offchain unhyphenated, "instruction handlers" for the functions and "instructions" for their input, "minor unit" not "base unit", one name per role enforced within each chapter, concrete two-asset examples, real handler names in lifecycle prose, persona-driven walkthroughs.
- Banned phrases, enforced by `scripts/lint-manuscript.mjs` (`npm run lint`) over the manuscript files: "the oracle problem", "instrument" (say "financial software" or "program"), the hyphenated chain words, and em-dashes. The lint also rejects negative framing of offchain truth (any "cannot verify the price" phrasing): the manuscript teaches the positive mechanism, checking a fact's provenance, freshness, and confidence against a source the program trusts. "Smart contract" is banned in favour of "program", with exactly two exceptions, both contrastive: the Introduction's line telling a reader arriving from another chain what a Solana program is, and the glossary's definition.
- An address is written as a markdown link, `[host.tld](https://host.tld)`, never as loose prose, because the PDF build only reaches links. It prints in the monospace face `program_id` uses, never underlined: underlining is a screen convention and collides with descenders in a serif face. A link whose text is words rather than an address gets that address printed after it in parentheses, since nobody can click a printed page. Both are done by `scripts/build-pdf.mjs`; the lint catches an address written as prose.
- The publisher is **Quicknode**, one word, that capitalization. The legal entity is **Quiknode Inc.**, and it appears only where the entity is what is meant: the copyright holder, the liability disclaimer, the trademark owner, and the exclusive right to sell editions. There is no "Quicknode Press" imprint. `scripts/lint-manuscript.mjs` guards the spellings across the manuscript; `LICENSE.md` and `README.md` sit outside its globs and are checked by eye.
- Numbers from outside the companion repository (fees, incident losses, market sizes) name their source in the text, link the primary publisher, and say where and when they apply. A figure that contradicts a reader's own experience is the normal case rather than the exception, because a cost that is typical in one country is unheard of in another: state the scope, so a reader whose transfers have all been domestic can check the claim in one click instead of concluding the book made it up.
- Nothing in the book disparages other Solana resources or projects. Where other content is referenced, it is as a neutral pointer, and preferably to a primary source (a project's own docs or repo) rather than to an aggregator or list.
- Chapter titles are numbered traditional short names: "Chapter 3: Tokens", "Chapter 11: Escrow", "Chapter 9: The Adversary". Headings *inside* a chapter carry the content, so a reader skimming only the headings learns the chapter's actual claims: "A Mint Is a Factory, and the Mint Authority Is Its Boss", not "Mint Authority". A heading is close to a summary of its section, and has to work read alone, because the printed table of contents lists every one of them out of context. It states what the reader gets, not the mechanism that delivers it: "Seeds Make Data Findable", not "Seeds Make Addresses Derivable", because derivability is how it works and findability is what it buys. Title Case throughout. No numbered headings.
- Headings never name characters, in any part of the book. A heading is close to a summary of its section and has to work read alone, so it states the general mechanism and the characters stay in the prose beneath: "A User's Public Key Is Their Address", not "Alice's Public Key Is Her Address"; "A Crossing Ask Fills Against the Resting Bid", not "Carol's Ask at 245 Crosses Bob's Bid". The program chapters are walkthroughs, so their headings still track the story's order and keep the specific mechanism ("The Shorted Asset Rallies and the Position Becomes Liquidatable"); it is the proper nouns that go, never the specificity. Where a role is what acts, name the role: the maker, the taker, the market authority, a liquidator. `scripts/lint-manuscript.mjs` enforces this across the manuscript.
- Handler names follow one rule across every program: **if the domain has a word for it, use the domain's word; if the handler only stamps out a container, it is `initialize_`.** The domain verbs are what make the code read like finance and they come in pairs, so `make_offer()`/`take_offer()`, `open_position()`/`close_position()`, `place_order()`/`cancel_order()`, `borrow`, `repay`, `liquidate`, `contribute`, `refund` all stay. Setup has no domain meaning and used to have four names (`init_`, `create_`, `initialize_`, and a bare `initialize`), so it is now always `initialize_x()`, naming what it initializes. The test is whether the handler moves money or takes a market action, or only writes parameters into a new account. A third family, `add_x()`, extends a container that already exists (`add_liquidity()`, `add_outcome()`, `add_asset()`) and is left alone.
- **PDA** names an address; **account** names the data sitting at it. They are not synonyms, so pick by what the sentence is about: PDA where the subject is derivation, seeds, authority, ownership, or signing ("the market PDA signs the transfers", "the offer PDA is derived from the maker's address"), and account where the subject is fields, balances, rent, creation, or closure ("the position account closes on exit", "each asset config account records a mint"). An account map lists accounts, so its caption says account, except for a dataless authority, which really is only an address. The shorthand "the config is a PDA (seed `"config"`) holding the admin" stays allowed where an account-model section is giving the seeds, because there the derivation is the point.
- A chapter's What You Learnt list states what the reader now knows, not what the characters did, so it is written generally even when the chapter that taught it was a story: "a user's account holds their balance of SOL", not "Alice's holds her balance". The same goes for its opening line.
- A section lives in the chapter its subject belongs to, not the chapter where it was first needed. If a section would sit equally well in a different chapter, it is in the wrong one: rent is a property of every account, so it belongs with accounts, even though PDAs is where the escrow's rent lifecycle first made it matter. Each concept is taught once, in full, in the chapter that owns it; everywhere else it gets a clause and that chapter's name, never a second explanation. When a section turns out to be two things, split it and send each half home.
- A forward reference names the thing being deferred, and never routes through a concept the reader has not met yet: "covered later in this part", never "covered with PDAs later in this part", because a reader meeting rent in the opening pages has never heard of a PDA and now has two unknowns instead of one. Most deferrals need no pointer at all: name the concept, say what it does in a clause, and move on.
- Cross-references in prose use chapter names ("the PDAs chapter", "The Adversary"), never numbers. Chapter numbers appear only in chapter titles and the table of contents, so renumbering never rewrites prose.
- Characters are introduced at the point they first act, never in a separate cast section.
- Boxouts (rendered as blockquotes) hold self-contained asides a reader can skip without losing the thread: War Stories, origin stories, and mechanism deep-dives like the flash-loan and keypair-collision boxes.
- Facts that form a grid go in a table (the cluster table, the token-extensions table, the rounding-direction table), not in a comma-packed paragraph.
- Plain language. Define things by what they concretely are, not by abstract category: an account is "some data - a Rust struct - with an address", never "the unit of state". If a definition would fit equally well in a book about a different subject, it is too vague. Prefer the short common word to the impressive one: "the deployed program", not "the deployed bytecode"; "compiled code", not "compiled bytecode". Reach for the precise technical term only where the precision is the point.
- Example assets are USDC and real-world assets: stocks like NVDAx, TSLAx, and SPCXx. Never meme tokens.
- Talk to readers, never about the book's construction. No sentences explaining why a chapter is organized, sized, or placed the way it is ("each section is short on purpose", "that is why it comes first"). If a sentence is addressed to the author, editor, or reviewer rather than the reader, delete it.
- Emphasis is meaningful. Titles of books and other works are italicized (*TCP/IP Illustrated*). Bold marks the load-bearing claim of a passage and canonical terms on first use ("the chain is the **platform**"). Use both wherever they help the reader, and never as decoration.
- The two companions have fixed names in the book's prose: solana-program-examples is "the companion repository" and solana-finance-claude-plugin is "the companion AI skill". Each is introduced once, with a link, in the Introduction; everywhere else the fixed name is used, never the repo name.

## The Program Chapter Template

Each program chapter runs the same eight beats:

- **Concept** - what this financial software does in the real world, with personas, real assets, and each party's incentive (the Introduction promises readers that admin and user incentives are discussed in every program chapter). Personas are introduced where they first act, not in a cast list.
- **Account model** - the accounts, their owners, their PDAs, with the account-relationship diagram.
- **Instruction handlers** - the lifecycle walked in prose, each handler named and explained at first use, each step followed by a diagram of what moved and what changed; no flat reference section.
- **The math** - the calculations, in minor units, with rounding directions stated.
- **The invariants** - what must hold before and after every handler.
- **The failure modes** - how this program gets attacked or bricked, with the inline War Story where an incident matches.
- **Testing considerations** - the property or scenario tests from the example's own suite that would catch each failure mode, described without dwelling on test libraries (the tooling is covered once, in Operating Financial Software).
- **What you learnt** - a closing summary of the concepts the chapter taught and the traditional-finance roles and processes the program replaced.

The chapters in the two Fundamentals sections (Solana Building Blocks; Financial Programming Concepts in Solana) use a lighter per-section template: claim, mechanism, consequence, War Story where applicable, and a short "What You Learnt" list closing each chapter.

## Generation Pipeline (per chapter)

- Gather inputs: the example's program source, README, and tests from the [companion repository](https://github.com/quicknode/solana-program-examples); the relevant rules from the companion AI skill; War Story primary sources.
- Draft against the template.
- Verify: grep every named identifier against the repo; check every number against the source it came from; run the banned-phrase lint.
- List the figures the chapter needs. Figures are drawn later in a single visual pass so the visual language stays consistent.
- Review against the companion AI skill's rules and the Glossary.

## The Visual Language

The diagram conventions (account-relationship maps, state machines, token-movement flows, the War Story box) are production guidance, not reader content, so they live here rather than in an appendix. They are fixed during the single visual pass that draws every figure, and each convention is taught to the reader by the first figure that uses it, with a small legend on that figure. One consistent set of conventions across the whole book; no figure invents its own.

Conventions already settled, which the visual pass must preserve:

- **The cluster curve is the part of the ball-shaped Ed25519 curve near X=0 — flat at the top-left, sloping gently down and to the right — and nothing crosses it.** Canonical path `M 0,36 Q 400,36 640,116`: monotone, never drooping down in the middle and never raising up in the middle (no domes). The gentle slope leaves room beneath the right side of the curve for the rightmost column and empty space in the top-right corner, where all titles go (cluster label right-aligned, e.g. `x="628" y="24"`). Every on-curve address dot (each person, each mint, the program) sits on the curve by the shared placement formula; every box hangs below with its top clearing the curve at the box's right edge by ≥6px. A mint's small box sits in the open top-right region (or the strip above the flat left), its dot on the curve beneath it. No arrow or leader line ever crosses the curve. The curve is the heaviest stroke in the figure (`stroke-width="3.5"`); token arrows and everything else stay visibly lighter.
- **No arrow or leader passes through a label.** A label sitting on its own arrow's path is the most common defect after re-anchoring; the white halo cannot hide a stroke crossing a word. Labels sit beside their arrows, never on them.
- **Struct boxes show real fields; text never escapes a box.** Every `key: value` line in a data-PDA box is a verbatim field from the chapter's struct definition with the story's value; notes that aren't fields are italic annotations. All text keeps ≥6px padding inside its rect — shrink the field/handler font in a crowded box rather than letting text touch or cross the border.
- **Accounts stack in columns beneath their owner's dot.** A person's column reads top to bottom: wallet, their token account(s), their per-program user account, their bets/orders/positions. Program-wide accounts stack beneath the program's dot: program box, config, event/market/pool PDA, outcomes/reserves, vaults. Box top positions stay fixed across every figure in a chapter.
- **People are wallet rectangles**: a bordered rectangle with a head-and-shoulders glyph, a bold left-aligned name title (role labels like "Alice (maker)" in program chapters, "Alice's Account" in the Fundamentals chapters), and the person's SOL balance beneath. Lamport and token arrows that touch a person start or end at this rectangle's edge, never at a bare curve dot or a stick figure. Instruction handlers listed inside a program's rectangle are left-aligned, one per line, always with parens: `place_bet()`.
- **Each account box carries its kind's icon** beside the title, from the fixed vocabulary: head-and-shoulders person for wallets, piggy bank for token accounts and vaults, gear for programs, table for data-struct PDAs, a columned mint building for mints. One vocabulary book-wide; the exact glyph paths live in the finance skill's DIAGRAMS.md.
- **Every address dot has an account rectangle.** A dot with nothing hanging off it says the address exists but has no account, which is never what a figure means. Wherever a dot is drawn, the account at that address is drawn too, rounded, with its icon and title, so a figure never teaches that one kind of address has accounts and another does not. The one exception is a legend key, where a lone dot beside its explanatory line is the whole point.
- **Rounded corners mean accounts, and nothing else.** Every box that is not an account (transaction envelopes, instruction rows, the transaction log, state-machine states, seed chips, failed or hypothetical calls, offchain parties, annotation panels) draws with square corners, so a reader can tell at a glance what lives on the chain as an account and what does not.
- **Address dots have white centers, on-curve and off-curve alike**: `fill="#fff" stroke="#111" stroke-width="2"`. A dot **on** the curve carries no label — sitting on the curve is what says the address is a public key. An off-curve dot carries its seed list and nothing else (`"OFFER" + MAKER'S ADDRESS + ID`), with no `SEEDS:` prefix. The deriving program is never in the label, because every seed list leaves it implied: an ATA reads `ALICE'S ADDRESS + TOKEN PROGRAM + USDC MINT`, its three real seeds, and not the Associated Token Program that derived it. The book teaches both conventions once, in prose, where Alice and Bob and their token accounts first appear.
- **Public-key accounts hug their dot, and each column packs under its own wallet.** Put the dot where the curve comes closest to the box (its right edge below the curve, its left edge above), so the box sits ~6px away rather than floating; if a box still can't reach its dot, shrink every font in the figure one step. Columns start at different heights, following their wallets down the curve — never pad a column to align a row across people.
- **Every account box reads icon, title, rule, fields.** Icon and title are centred on each other, with a hairline beneath them across the box's inner width. In a step figure, any value that step changes is **bold** — PDA field lines and token balances alike.
- **Tokens move directly between accounts.** An arrow never stops on a panel and resumes; fees, gates and math go in an italic annotation beside it, and a field a step changes is shown as that field's value inside its own PDA, never as a floating callout. An arrow's label is the amount actually transferred.
- **Arrows are for movement, so instruction routing gets none.** A transaction box lists its instructions and each row names the program it is addressed to ("Instruction 1 → System Program"); no arrow is drawn from those rows to the programs or accounts the instruction touches. An arrow in these figures says something moved, and a second kind of arrow pointing at every account a handler writes sends the reader hunting for a transfer that never happened. Connectors that are not token movement (lamports, rent, authority, a program's write into the transaction log) stay dashed grey.
- **Seeds are never drawn inside an account's rectangle**, because seeds are not part of the account struct. They are labeled near the small address circle that sits above or beside the account box.
- **A custom PDA's rectangle shows its struct as `key: value`, one field per line**, using the walkthrough's story values where the chapter has them.
- **Every walkthrough step ("Alice does X", "Bob does Y") is followed by a diagram** showing the token movements, account creations and closures, and value changes that step caused.
- **Per-step diagrams show the program's whole account picture, fading what the step doesn't touch down to its heading.** The chapter's account map fixes the column layout; each step figure reuses those positions. Accounts the step reads or writes draw at full ink; every untouched account is wrapped in `<g opacity="0.3">` and reduced to icon plus title — no struct fields, no balances. One fade level, `0.3`. Accounts that don't exist yet at that point in the story are omitted, not faded.
- **One accent green, on the step's action only.** The palette is ink on paper plus `#1e7a3c`, reserved for the invoked handler line, token-movement arrows with their coins and amount labels, and NEW badges. Accented marks are also the boldest, so figures read identically in greyscale print; no second hue anywhere.
- **Token movement arrows carry coins.** A solid accent arrow with a bold accent amount label, and a coin mark (three overlapping white-filled rings, no currency symbol) immediately beside that label. The rings carry no `$`, because the arrows carry ACME, NVDAx, SPCXx, LP tokens and SOL as often as they carry USDC — the ticker in the label names the asset, and the mark only says that tokens move. Three rings rather than two so the mark cannot be read as a pair of address dots at print size. Exactly one coin per amount label: the coin marks the stated quantity, so an arrow that moves value without stating an amount needs the amount added, not a coin left floating. Lamport, rent, and authority relationships stay dashed grey with no coins and no accent, so a reader scanning for value transfer follows the green.
- **An arrow is one smooth stroke, and its head points along it.** Where two Bézier segments join, the handles either side of the anchor are collinear, so the flow never shows a corner mid-route. The head is rotated to the tangent at the path's very last point, so the final stretch has to be travelling in the head's direction already — a short terminal hook swings the head off the line and the stroke comes out through its flank instead of meeting the middle of its base.
- **Handler names carry parens in prose too**: `settle_event()` invokes, `event` is an account, `total_pool` is a field. Test names stay bare.

## Reviewers, Feedback, and Editions

- **Named technical reviewers**: recruit respected Solana engineers early, so review happens per part rather than as one giant pass at the end. Their names fill the About the Technical Reviewers section. Visible peer review is part of how fundamentals titles earn their standing.
- **Public pre-print feedback loop**: release chapters early, as posts or an early-access build, collect corrections, and credit contributors in Acknowledgements.
- **Errata and editions**: a public errata page from day one, and a stated edition cadence. The durable layer changes slowly, but "long-lived" is only credible if the maintenance process is visible.
